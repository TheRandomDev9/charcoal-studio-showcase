Spaces fall short

A model does not receive a sentence as readable text.
It needs a sequence of numbers tied to pieces in the tokenizer’s vocabulary.
Consider “Héllò, annoyingly!” How can punctuation, an accent, and a possibly unfamiliar word become those numbers?
Splitting only at spaces gives two chunks, each with punctuation still attached.
And assigning one number to every possible word and spelling would require an enormous vocabulary.

Text to IDs

Instead, the tokenizer converts the text in stages.
First, this example’s configured cleanup rules remove accents and lowercase letters, turning “Héllò” into “hello.”
Next, splitting around spaces and punctuation establishes regions for “hello,” the comma, “annoyingly,” and the exclamation mark.
These regions are not necessarily the final tokens.
Learned rules divide them into pieces found in a fixed vocabulary.
Suppose this vocabulary contains “hello,” “annoying,” and “ly,” but not “annoyingly” as a whole.
Because the whole word is absent, it becomes the two known pieces “annoying” and “ly.” Reusing smaller pieces lets unfamiliar words remain representable.
Every final token, including punctuation, is then looked up in the vocabulary, which supplies one ID for each token.

Returned encoding

A final configured step may add special tokens that were not in the sentence.
If the target format expects start and end markers, they are added before the encoding is returned.
The result now contains one ID each for the start marker, “hello,” comma, “annoying,” “ly,” exclamation mark, and end marker.
Now replace “annoyingly” with “hello.” Because this vocabulary stores “hello” as one token, the replacement uses one ID rather than two.
Another tokenizer may split the same text differently because its cleanup rules and vocabulary can differ.
The tokenizer’s job ends with this returned encoding; what the model does with those IDs comes next.
